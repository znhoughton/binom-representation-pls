\begin{abstract}
Humans balance abstract and item-specific knowledge when ordering
binomials: abstract constraints shape the preferences for infrequent
binomials, while idiosyncratic knowledge drives them for frequent ones.
Evaluating 16 language models across five families, we demonstrate a
parallel trade-off. Probing hidden-state representations shows that
language models do learn abstract preferences. However, a binomial's
relative frequency in the training corpus predicts a model's preference
over and above anything those abstract preferences supply, and predicts
it more strongly for high-frequency binomials. To locate that
item-specific component, we zero out cross-word attention, forcing the
representation into a strictly additive function of the two words. The
intervention mitigates but does not remove the idiosyncrasy of
high-frequency binomials, indicating that the lexical representations
themselves come to represent some amount of the phrase-level
representation as well. Examining the developmental trajectory across
checkpoints further shows that abstract preferences become measurable
earlier in training than the frequency-dependent storage does, at a
point governed by training tokens rather than model scale.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{introduction}

Language users are constantly faced with a trade-off between
holistically stored, item-specific knowledge and abstract, generative
knowledge. For example, the meaning of a phrase often depends on whether
it is composed from its individual components or retrieved as a whole.
Idioms, such as \emph{kicked the bucket}, are the clearest form of this:
the compositional meaning refers to the action of kicking a bucket
whereas the idiomatic meaning refers to someone passing away. More
generally, the representation of a multi-word expression may be composed
from the representations of its individual pieces, or the expression may
be stored and retrieved holistically. How, and crucially when, this
trade-off emerges in learning has been a question that linguists have
been asking for nearly a century
\citep{morgan2015, morgan2024productiveknowledgeitemspecific, houghton2023doespredictabilitydrive, houghton2024frequencydependentpreferenceextremity, houghton2025boltsnutslanguage, houghton2026exemplarsdisguisepure}.

\subsection{Abstract Preferences and Holistic
Storage}\label{abstract-preferences-and-holistic-storage}

Historically, these questions have been largely addressed at the
behavioral level. \citet{berkoChildsLearningEnglish1958} famously
demonstrated that children could produce the correct plural of nouns
that they had never heard before rather early in life; because the
plural form was never available to be stored, it must instead have been
generated using abstract knowledge of the language. Evidence for
abstract knowledge of this kind has been relatively uncontroversial, and
has been demonstrated at many different levels of language. The priming
literature, for instance, has demonstrated that sentences sharing
syntactic structure
\citep[e.g.,][]{bock1986syntacticpersistencelanguage} or semantic
structure \citep[e.g.,][]{meyer1971facilitationrecognizingpairs}
facilitate the processing of later sentences sharing that structure,
which requires that the shared structure be represented independently of
the particular words that realize it.

Evidence for holistic storage, on the other hand, has been somewhat more
controversial. Nonetheless, evidence of holistic storage has been
accumulating over the last few decades
\citep{stembergerFrequencyLexicalStorage1986, bybee1999usagebasedphonology, stembergerAreInflectedForms2004, kapatsinskiFrequencyEmergencePrefabs2009, raymondBrown2012frequency, morgan2015, morgan2016, raymondBrownHealy2016cumulative, morgan2024productiveknowledgeitemspecific, houghton2025multiwordrepresentationsminds}.
Perhaps the most famous example comes from
\citet{bybeeEffectUsageDegrees1999}. In their seminal study, they
demonstrated that the extent to which the word \emph{don't} is reduced
is determined by the frequency of the phrase it occurs in. \emph{I don't
know} can be reduced to a single nasalized vowel, while \emph{don't} in
less frequent phrases, such as \emph{I don't walk}, is unable to be
reduced as much. The context-specificity of this reduction demonstrates
that the representation of certain high-frequency phrases cannot be
reduced to the simple composition of the representations of the
individual words.

Binomial expressions (e.g., \emph{cats and dogs}) are notable for their
relationship between abstract preferences and holistic storage: they
show evidence of being driven by both kinds of knowledge. Speakers have
strong intuitions about the order of the two words: \emph{salt and
pepper} sounds far more natural than \emph{pepper and salt}, even though
there's nothing ungrammatical about the latter. For lower frequency
binomials, \citet{morgan2016} demonstrated that these preferences are
driven by abstract constraints such as the length of a word, the
semantic content of a word, etc \citep{benor2006}. However, that same
study also showed that those constraints don't account for the ordering
preferences of high-frequency binomials like \emph{salt and pepper}. For
those binomials, the preferences can't be predicted solely from the
properties of the individual words, suggesting that the binomial is
instead stored \emph{holistically}. Binomial ordering preferences
therefore show sensitivity to both abstract preferences and holistic
storage depending on their frequency, which makes them a particularly
useful case for studying the trade-off between the two.

\subsection{Abstract Preferences in Language
Models}\label{abstract-preferences-in-language-models}

Language models offer a way to study this trade-off that behavioral work
cannot. Questions about the timecourse in which an expression stops
being formed compositionally and starts being stored holistically is
fundamentally one about experience, but a human speaker's history with
any particular expression can only be estimated reasonably using corpus
frequency. In a language model, that history is the training corpus
itself, and it can be counted exactly. More importantly, in language
models the representations are also directly inspectable, and the
computation that produces them can be intervened on. Neither is possible
for human learners.

The evidence for abstract preferences and holistic storage in language
models has been more mixed than in the human behavioral literature.
Models clearly generalize beyond what they have seen:
\citet{misraLanguageModelsLearn2024} show that language models can
produce the rare \emph{a beautiful five days} construction even when it
is withheld from training, generalizing to it from related constructions
that are better attested, and \citet{yaoBothDirectIndirect2025} find
that dative alternation preferences are shaped by abstract constraints.
Beyond text models, there is also evidence of abstractions for automatic
speech recognition models as well \citep{pluth2026mechanistic}. At the
same time, models retain a great deal of item-specific knowledge.
\citet{mccoy2023howmuchlanguage} find that while model-generated text is
often as structurally novel as human text, the same models also
reproduce passages of over a thousand words verbatim from their training
data. As such, it is clear that both abstract preferences and holistic
storage are clearly at play within language models, however what remains
unclear is how these types of knowledge trade off and when they emerge
during training.

Perhaps most related to the present study, language models' ordering
preferences for binomials appear not to be driven by abstract
preferences at all. \citet{houghton2025role} examined whether language
models showed the same abstract preferences as humans, and found that
the models' ordering preferences were driven exclusively by frequency
statistics, with no evidence of an effect of abstract preferences.
However, that finding faces limitations along two main dimensions.
First, the study can only claim to find no evidence that the models'
ordering preferences were driven by \emph{human} ordering preferences;
it may very well be that their preferences are driven by abstract
preferences that simply differ from those humans show. Second, the
examination was conducted on model behavior, in the form of output
probabilities, rather than over the representations that produce it. A
model could in principle derive an ordering from abstract properties of
the binomial and still produce output statistics that look
frequency-driven.

\subsection{Present Study}\label{present-study}

The present study thus asks whether binomial ordering preferences in
language models are driven by abstract structure or if they are simply
holistically stored. We make two changes to the design of
\citet{houghton2025role}. First, rather than testing model behavior
against a fixed inventory of human constraints, we ask whether
\emph{any} systematic structure is recoverable at all, by training a
probe to predict a model's ordering preference from its hidden
representations and evaluating it on binomials that the probe was never
trained on. Second, we separate the two components architecturally
rather than statistically. Zeroing out cross-word attention forces the
model's preference to be purely compositional: a strictly additive
function of the individual words evaluated separately. Interventions on
attention have been used to trace factual recall and to isolate circuits
\citep{geva-etal-2023-dissecting, wang2023interpretability}, but to our
knowledge this is the first use of one to separate holistic storage from
abstract preferences. We apply this to 16 models across five families,
spanning human-scale to trillion-token training corpora, and to training
checkpoints for two families matched in parameters.

Our main contributions are:

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{Empirical evidence for both abstract preferences and
  frequency-driven holistic storage.} We show that a probe trained on
  LLM hidden-state representations for binomials can predict the
  ordering preferences for binomials it hasn't seen, however the
  binomial's relative frequency in the training data predicts a model's
  preference over and above anything those abstract preferences predict,
  and does so more strongly the more often the model encountered it.
\item
  \textbf{An architectural intervention for isolating abstract
  preferences.} Zeroing out cross-word attention forces the
  representation into a strictly additive function of the two words, so
  the probe can only use what each word carries on its own.
\item
  \textbf{Evidence that storage shifts lexical, not only phrasal,
  representations.} Zeroing out cross-word attention mitigates but does
  not eliminate the item-specific knowledge, indicating that repeated
  exposure to a phrase also changes the individual lexical
  representations themselves.
\end{itemize}

\section{Methods}\label{sec-methods}

All three experiments apply the same procedure and differ only in which
models\footnote{Appendix \ref{sec-appendix-models} lists all sixteen
  models with their parameter counts and training-token estimates.} and
checkpoints it is applied to. This section describes that procedure: the
datasets, the probes, and the analyses.\codefootnote

\subsection{Datasets}\label{datasets}

In order to examine the ordering preferences for binomials, we created
two binomial corpora: a novel set and an attested set. The \textbf{novel
set} comprises approximately 340,000 binomials from the Wikitext corpus
\citep{merity2016pointer} selected to be absent from BabyLM's training
corpus. The \textbf{attested corpus set} comprises approximately 49,000
binomials from the BabyLM corpus \citep{warstadt-etal-2023-findings}.

The novel set is genuinely novel only for the BabyLM models; for the
large-scale models it consists of infrequent attested binomials rather
than unseen ones. We retain the label \emph{novel} throughout
nonetheless.

Both sets were extracted automatically using a constituent parser
requiring the two words to form a minimal coordinate phrase
(Appendix~\ref{sec-appendix-extraction}).

For each binomial and each model, we also calculate that model's
ordering preference for the binomial, which is operationalized as the
model's own log-probability ratio:
\(y_{\text{true}} = \log P(W_1\text{ and }W_2) - \log P(W_2\text{ and }W_1)\).

For the developmental analysis in Experiment 3, we probe seven
checkpoints from the BabyLM and Pythia models, the first being the
untrained initialization. BabyLM checkpoints were sampled at
approximately even intervals across training; Pythia checkpoints were
instead chosen so that each model had seen roughly the same number of
tokens as the corresponding BabyLM checkpoints. The trained checkpoints
span roughly 20M to 2B effective training tokens.

\subsection{Representation Extraction}\label{representation-extraction}

For each model we extract final-layer hidden states, presenting each
binomial in both orderings (\emph{W}\textsubscript{1} and
\emph{W}\textsubscript{2}; \emph{W}\textsubscript{2} and
\emph{W}\textsubscript{1}) within the same sentence frame. Each binomial
is represented in two ways:

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{Binomial-level}:
  \([\bar{h}(W_1\text{ and }W_2); \bar{h}(W_2\text{ and }W_1)]\), where
  \(\bar{h}\) denotes the mean of token representations across all
  tokens in the binomial in each ordering.
\item
  \textbf{Word-level}: \([h(W_1); h(W_2)]\), where \(h(W_k)\) is the
  hidden state of word \(W_k\) taken from the sentence in which it
  appears in first position.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Attention Conditions}\label{attention-conditions}

We additionally probe representations under two attention conditions:

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{Full context}: standard forward pass with full cross-token
  attention. Each word's representation reflects contextual influences
  from all other (previous) tokens in the sentence.
\item
  \textbf{Attention-zeroed}: the same forward pass, except that we zero
  out attention from the second word and the conjunction to the first
  word, at every layer (Appendix~\ref{sec-appendix-implementation}).
\end{itemize}

This gives three representation types per model: binomial-level under
each attention condition, and full-context word-level.

Because \(y_{\text{true}}\) is a difference of log-probabilities over
the two orderings, and because causal masking already prevents \(W_1\)
from attending to \(W_2\), blocking attention from \(W_2\) and the
conjunction to \(W_1\) leaves the model blind to the other word. The
resulting preference is necessarily of the form \(g(W_1) - g(W_2)\): an
additive function of the two words evaluated separately, with no
interaction term. The attention-zeroed model therefore reports the
preference it would hold given only word-level information.

\subsection{Probes}\label{probes}

For each representation type, we train a two-layer MLP regressor (hidden
dimension 128, ReLU activation) to predict binomial ordering preferences
from the binomial representations. The probe takes the concatenated
representations for a binomial and outputs a single continuous value
\(\hat{y}\), trained under mean-squared-error loss against
\(y_{\text{true}}\), with antisymmetric augmentation so that a swapped
ordering carries the negated label. Optimizer settings, early stopping,
and the augmentation rate are given in
Appendix~\ref{sec-appendix-implementation}. We report cross-validated R²
(the squared Pearson correlation between \(\hat{y}\) and
\(y_{\text{true}}\) on held-out folds), averaged across 10
folds.\footnote{The results below do not depend on the MLP's capacity. A
  ridge regression on the same data, using the same folds and a single
  tuned hyperparameter, recovers most of the MLP's performance. Full
  comparison in Appendix~\ref{sec-appendix-linear}.}

We evaluate generalization under two 10-fold cross-validation
conditions:

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{Binomial-held-out}: binomials are held out as units; each
  binomial in the test set is excluded from the training set, however
  overlapping words are allowed (e.g., \emph{radios and computers} is
  not excluded from the training set even if \emph{radios and
  televisions} is in the test set).
\item
  \textbf{Word-held-out}: binomials with overlapping words are excluded
  from the training set. For example, if \emph{radios and televisions}
  is in the test set, then \emph{radios and computers} is excluded from
  the training set.
\end{itemize}

Both splits are applied to the novel set; the attested set is not
cross-validated, and is instead used to evaluate the effects of binomial
frequency on the probe's predictions. Two senses of novelty are in play
here, and they are nested. The novel set is novel with respect to the
\emph{language model's} training corpus, in that the pairing does not
occur there; the two splits then control what the \emph{probe} has seen
within that set. An item in the word-held-out test set is thus new to
the probe in both its pairing and its two words, while those same words
are ordinary vocabulary that the language model encountered many times.
We report fold standard deviations throughout
(Appendix~\ref{sec-appendix-cv}).

Finally, following \citet{hewitt-liang-2019-designing}, we train a
matched control probe on randomly permuted labels; near-zero control R²
indicates the probe is recovering linguistic structure rather than
overfitting to noise.

\subsection{Analyses}\label{sec-methods-analyses}

We report two measurements. The first is the probe's cross-validated
\(R^2\) under the two splits described prevously, of which word-held-out
is the stricter (since binomial-held-out \(R^2\) may be inflated by
general lexical preferences).

The second measurement evaluates the probe on the attested set. Since
the probe has seen only binomials the model has not encountered (or
encountered rarely in the case of the larger models), its prediction
\(\hat{y}\) for an attested binomial estimates what abstract knowledge
alone would predict, and cannot reflect the probe (or the LLM) having
memorized that binomial. In order to analyze the extent to which the
model's ordering preference within the attested set is driven by
abstract vs holistic knowledge, we implement a Bayesian regression model
using \emph{brms} \citep{burkner2017brms} with default priors. Our
dependent variable is \(y_{\text{true}}\), the model's ordering
preference. The probe's prediction, \(\hat{y}\), stands in for abstract
preferences. Relative frequency, \(\text{RelFreq}\), is the centered
proportion of occurrences in alphabetical ordering: \(0.5\) means the
binomial always occurs in alphabetical ordering in the corpus, \(0\)
means it occurs evenly in both orderings, and \(-0.5\) means it always
occurs in non-alphabetical ordering. Letting \(\text{log(Frequency)}\)
be log overall frequency (overall occurrences in the model's corpus,
logged\footnote{Frequency is each binomial's exposure in the model's own
  training corpus: exact counts for BabyLM
  \citep{warstadt-etal-2023-findings} and Pythia, and infini-gram Pile
  counts \citep{gao2020pile, liu2024infinigram} standing in as a proxy
  for the rest. Appendix~\ref{sec-appendix-corpus-freq} gives the
  distributions.}), our regression equation is:

\begin{equation}\phantomsection\label{eq-relfreq}{
\begin{aligned}
& y_{\text{true}} \sim \hat{y} * \text{log(Frequency)} \\ & \quad + \text{RelFreq} * \text{log(Frequency)} \\ & \log(\sigma) \sim \text{log(Frequency)}
\end{aligned}
}\end{equation}

\(y_{\text{true}}\) and \(\hat{y}\) are centered and scaled within each
fit; \(\text{log(Frequency)}\) is centered but not scaled;
\(\text{RelFreq}\) is left on its original scale, which already runs
from \(-0.5\) to \(+0.5\) (Appendix~\ref{sec-appendix-relfreq}).

The residual scale is modeled rather than assumed constant. The spread
of \(y_{\text{true}}\) varies with frequency, and an unmodeled trend
there would inflate the slopes at one end of the range.

\section{Experiment 1: BabyLM Representations}\label{sec-exp1}

In this first experiment, we probe three BabyLMs (125M, 350M, and 1.3B
parameters)\footnote{The BabyLM models used here are OPT-based models
  trained for 20 epochs on the BabyLM v3 corpus
  \citep{houghton2026holisticstorageverbupphrases, warstadt-etal-2023-findings}.}
at their final training checkpoint. Because these models were trained on
an amount of data comparable to humans, and because the novel set
excludes binomials in their training corpus, they give the cleanest test
of whether a language model encodes abstract binomial ordering
preferences.

\subsection{Results}\label{results}

\subsubsection{Probe Performance}\label{probe-performance}

Results are shown in Figure~\ref{fig-r2-babylm}; full numerical results
are in Appendix~\ref{sec-appendix-exp1} (Table~\ref{tbl-r2-babylm}). The
control probe R² values were consistently near zero across all
conditions and all three models (reported alongside each R² in
Table~\ref{tbl-r2-babylm}), confirming that the probe doesn't recover
spurious signal from noise.

For the binomial-level conditions, binomial-held-out R² ranges from 0.04
to 0.13; word-held-out R² is consistently lower, ranging from 0.04 to

preference signal is tied to word identity rather than being fully

attention conditions, from 0.13 at 125M to 0.07 at 1.3B under full
context, and the full-context condition yields higher binomial-held-out
R² than attention-zeroed in all three models.

\subsubsection{Abstract and Stored
Contributions}\label{abstract-and-stored-contributions}

Results are shown in Figure~\ref{fig-relfreq-babylm}. Both abstract
preferences and relative corpus frequency predict the model's ordering
preference, and both do so credibly in all three models
(Appendix~\ref{sec-appendix-relfreq}). The effect of relative frequency
is stronger for high-frequency binomials, while the effect of abstract
preferences is largely flat across frequencies, declining credibly only
in the 350M.

Zeroing out attention reduces both the effects of abstract preferences
and relative frequency for the BabyLMs, and makes the interaction
between abstract preferences and overall frequency credibly negative in
all three, suggesting that abstract preferences are a slightly
\emph{worse} predictor of ordering preferences for high-frequency
binomials when attention is zeroed.

\section{Experiment 2: Cross-Family Final-Layer
Representations}\label{sec-exp2}

Experiment 1 demonstrates that binomial ordering preferences within the
BabyLMs are driven by both abstract preferences and holistic storage.
Those models are small in both parameters and training data, so
Experiment 2 applies the same probe to 13 further models across four
large-scale families: Pythia, GPT-2, OLMo, and Llama, with the BabyLM
results carried over for comparison

Contributions}\label{abstract-and-stored-contributions-1}

Results are shown in Figure~\ref{fig-relfreq-pile}. Similar to the
BabyLMs, there is an effect of both abstract preferences and relative
frequency for all models. Further, the effect of relative frequency is
stronger for frequent binomials compared to infrequent ones. Unlike the
BabyLMs, however, the effect of abstract preferences \emph{decreases} as
a function of frequency. In other words, the more the models encounter a
binomial, the more their ordering preferences are governed by that
binomial's frequency and the less by abstract preferences.

Zeroing out attention mitigates the effect of relative frequency
substantially for both the main-effect and the interaction with overall
frequency, however both remain credible. Further, the effect of abstract
preferences \emph{increases} in nine of the thirteen large-scale models;
of the four exceptions, all of them among the largest, only Llama-3-8B
decreases credibly, the other three falling within their credible
intervals. The interaction effect grows more negative in all but one. In
other words, the effect of abstract preferences is generally stronger
with attention zeroed out, but this increase is weaker for
high-frequency binomials.

Notably, the effect of zeroing out attention on abstract preferences
differs between the BabyLMs and most of the other models. For the
BabyLMs, zeroing out attention weakens the effect of both abstract
preferences and relative frequency; for most of the large-scale models,
it weakens the effect of relative frequency but \emph{strengthens} the
effect of abstract preferences. The reduction in the effect of relative
frequency, by contrast, is of a similar size in the two groups, ranging
from 33\% to 42\% in the BabyLMs against 30\% to 47\% in the others. We
return to possible explanations for this contrast in the Discussion and

\section{Experiment 3: Developmental Trajectories}\label{sec-exp3}

Experiments 1 and 2 establish that language models encode binomial
preferences at the end of training, but leave open when those
representations emerge, and whether the trade-off between abstract
preferences and holistic storage is present early or accumulates with
exposure.

The probe, inputs, attention conditions, and cross-validation splits are
as described previously. We refit Equation~\ref{eq-relfreq} at each
saved checkpoint of the three BabyLM models and the three Pythia models,
in both attention conditions.

\subsection{Results}\label{results-2}

Probe trajectories are shown in Figure~\ref{fig-checkpoints} and the two
sources' contributions in Figure~\ref{fig-ckpt-sources}. Full numerical
and word-level results are in Appendix~\ref{sec-appendix-exp3}.
Binomial-held-out R² begins at essentially zero in every model and rises
with training. For the BabyLMs it is 0.00 at the earliest checkpoint
(\textasciitilde20M tokens), climbs to 0.13--0.14 by mid-training, and
stands at 0.10--0.14 at the end. Pythia follows the same course, rising
from 0.00--0.01 at 33.6M tokens to 0.15--0.19 at 2097M tokens. Full
context exceeds attention-zeroing at the final checkpoint in all three
BabyLMs, while in Pythia the two conditions end close together.

The effect of relative frequency is small across the first three
checkpoints of every model, never exceeding 0.29 (compared to a maximum
value of 1.03) across training. From the fourth checkpoint it is
credibly positive in every model and climbs thereafter to 0.353, 0.447
and 0.512 in the three BabyLM models, and to 0.995, 0.914 and 1.031 in
the three Pythia models. This holds in both attention conditions, so
what grows is not solely the binomial-specific representation. At step
0, before the models have seen any data, the effect is indistinguishable
from zero in the three BabyLM models, but credibly positive in the three
Pythia models (0.11--0.15); this Pythia floor is an artifact of subword
tokenization rather than any ordering preference in the untrained
networks.\footnote{This is a tokenization artifact: for some binomials
  the two orderings segment into different numbers of tokens, and at
  initialization each token costs roughly the log of the vocabulary
  size, so the difference between them is large for reasons that have
  nothing to do with ordering preferences. Where the preceding context
  ends in a quotation mark rather than a space, for instance, the first
  word loses its leading space, and \emph{``bread and butter''} is three
  tokens (\texttt{bread}, \texttt{and}, \texttt{butter}) while
  \emph{``butter and bread''} is four (\texttt{but}, \texttt{ter},
  \texttt{and}, \texttt{bread}). Roughly 2\% of items are affected.
  Excluding them eliminates the effect of relative frequency at the step
  0 checkpoint but leaves it unchanged in the fully trained models;
  Appendix \ref{sec-appendix-tokenization} gives the full comparison.}

At the fourth checkpoint, the first at which relative frequency is
credibly positive in every model, attention-zeroing removes none of the
effect. In other words, at the point where storage is first measurable,
it survives an intervention that removes the phrasal representation, so
what has shifted at that point is the lexical representations. The
phrasal representations shift afterwards. This is demonstrated from the
amount of the relative-frequency effect that is removed from zeroing out
cross attention. After the fourth checkpoint, the amount that the the
relative-frequency effect removed by zeroing out attention rises from
nothing at the fourth checkpoint to 13--21\% by the last in the three
Pythia models and 27--42\% in the three BabyLM models, and reaches
30--47\% in the fully trained large-scale models. The interaction effect
between relative frequency and overall frequency follows the same
course, reaching 0.191, 0.288 and 0.351 in the BabyLMs and 0.121, 0.104
and 0.130 in the Pythia models.

The effect of abstract preferences follows its own course. It rises
across training in both families and in both conditions, from near zero
at the earliest checkpoints to 0.374, 0.364 and 0.324 in the three
BabyLM models and 0.310, 0.326 and 0.327 in the three Pythia models,
with most cells reaching their maximum a checkpoint or two before the
end. A small effect is nonetheless present at step 0 under full context,
before the models have seen any data, though not under
attention-zeroing.\footnote{Novel binomials are novel as pairings, not
  as words: 97\% of attested binomials have both of their words
  somewhere in the probe's training set. A randomly initialized network
  already assigns each word an arbitrary but consistent bias, so the
  probe can learn those biases and carry them over to attested pairs
  built from the same words, and the effect scales with that overlap,
  from \(+0.12\) where the probe had seen both words to a non-credible
  estimate where it had seen neither. It accounts for roughly 1\% of the
  variance at step 0 against 17\% in the trained model.} Its interaction
with overall frequency is credibly negative under attention-zeroing in
17 of the 36 model-checkpoint fits (six checkpoints for each of the six
models).

Both sources are coefficients in the same regression, fit to the same
items, so we can ask when each becomes measurable on a single scale. We
use two criteria. The first is detectability: the earliest checkpoint
whose posterior sits credibly above the same coefficient's value at step
0, in the same model and condition, and stays there. Taking step 0
rather than zero as the baseline keeps an untrained network from
registering, which matters here because both coefficients are already
non-zero before training for reasons that have nothing to do with
learning. The second is trajectory: the earliest checkpoint at which a
coefficient has covered three quarters of its own change from step 0 to
its final value. The two fail in opposite directions, the first favoring
whichever measure is more sensitive and the second ignoring sensitivity
altogether.

Abstract preferences come first on both counts. They are detectable from
20 to 134M tokens against 118 to 315M for relative frequency, and cover
three quarters of their change by 118 to 1074M against 1074 to 2097M.
The interaction between relative frequency and overall frequency is last
on both, detectable from 298 to 1074M tokens. Per-model values are in
Appendix \ref{sec-appendix-timing}. We read this as soft evidence about
ordering rather than as an estimate of when either source becomes
available; the two measures are not on equal footing, for reasons we
return to in the Limitations.

\section{Discussion and Conclusion}\label{discussion-and-conclusion}

The present study examined whether language models' binomial ordering
preferences are driven by abstract structure, and if so, how that
structure emerges throughout training. Across 16 models from five
families, we found evidence for ordering preferences being driven by
both abstract and item-specific knowledge. Preferences generalize to
binomials that are absent from the language model's training corpus, and
continue to do so when the probe is additionally denied both of a
binomial's words during its own training, demonstrating abstract
structure that is shared across independently trained models. At the
same time, a binomial's relative frequency contributes over and above
abstract preferences, and contributes more as the binomial becomes more
frequent. These two sources also behave differently over training:
abstract preferences become measurable earlier than the item-specific
contribution does (though note that only being able to measure something
later does not mean the information wasn't present earlier). Finally,
zeroing out cross-word attention removes only part of the
relative-frequency contribution, suggesting that storage shifts the
lexical representations in addition the phrasal one.

The evidence for abstract preferences is particularly strong because it
does not depend on generalization across familiar words alone. The probe
recovers a model's preference for binomials that are absent from the
language model's own training corpus, and it does so even when both of
the binomial's words are additionally withheld from the probe's training
folds. The two exclusions operate at different levels: the pairing is
new to the language model, and the words are new to the probe. The
language model has of course seen those words many times. A ridge probe
with a single tuned hyperparameter recovers the same pattern, showing
that the result does not depend on the MLP's capacity
(Appendix~\ref{sec-appendix-linear}). The answer to the question left
open by \citet{houghton2025role} is therefore that models do have
abstract preferences, but ones that modeling with human constraints
seems to have failed to find. Further evidence that these preferences
are systematic comes without the probe at all: independently trained
models converge on the same preferences for the same novel binomials
(\(r = 0.50\), against a shuffled baseline of \(r \approx 0.00\)). This
agreement holds even among the three BabyLM models, which share a
training corpus but differ in capacity, and is stronger within model
families than between families, though it remains high between families
as well. Because all sixteen models received the same sentence frame,
the latter pattern cannot be attributed to the frame itself.

Abstract structure, however, does not fully determine a model's
preference for a particular binomial. A binomial's relative frequency
predicts the model's preference over and above what the abstract
structure predicts, and this item-specific contribution increases with
frequency. Unlike the abstract structure, this information is specific
to the particular binomial. The result therefore reveals a
frequency-dependent division between generalizable structure and
experience with individual items, closely paralleling the trade-off
between abstract preferences and item-specific experience observed by
\citet{morgan2016} in humans.

Abstract preferences come first. Probe R² begins at essentially zero and
rises over training, and in Equation~\ref{eq-relfreq} the abstract
component becomes measurable earlier than the item-specific one in every
model, on both of the criteria we apply to them. The item-specific
contribution follows a different trajectory: it is negligible through
the first three checkpoints of every model, becomes credibly positive
everywhere from the fourth, and then climbs monotonically under full
context without leveling off. This is not conclusive evidence against
item-specific knowledge being learned early, however, since the
measurement relies on a frequency-difference in representations to
detect holistic storage. The absence of a frequency difference,
consequently, does not necessarily indicate lack of storage.

Finally, the attention intervention provides further insight into how
this item-specific information is represented. Zeroing out cross-word
attention mitigates but does not eliminate the contribution of relative
frequency: at the first checkpoint where a binomial's relative frequency
predicts the model's ordering preference at all, attention-zeroing
removes none of that prediction; the share removed then increases over
training. This pattern suggests that item-specific information is not
exclusively stored as a holistic representation of the binomial.
Instead, it seems possible that repeated exposure to a binomial
gradually shifts the representations of its individual words, so that a
word encountered overwhelmingly in first position comes to encode
something of that tendency on its own. Such information remains
item-specific in that it concerns particular words and their learned
positional tendencies rather than a general constraint, but it no longer
requires the model to represent the specific binomial context. On this
interpretation, entrenchment shifts the lexical representations before
it shifts the phrasal one. This interpretation parallels findings in the
human behavioral literature showing that words experienced frequently in
specific contexts can come to reflect that change even when encountered
outside those contexts \citep{kapatsinski2026lexical}.

Overall, the findings bridge a longstanding gap between artificial
language models and usage-based accounts of language. Such accounts have
long proposed that frequent expressions become increasingly entrenched,
with behavioral work on binomials showing that human ordering
preferences reflect both abstract constraints and holistic storage. The
present results show a similar division emerging in a system with no
innate linguistic preferences: trained only to predict text, the models
learn both abstract representations and holistically stored
representations. The results therefore suggest that the interaction they
don't need separate mechanisms explicitly built into a learner, but
rather they can emerge from exposure itself within a distributed
representational system.

\section*{Limitations}\label{limitations}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{Limitations}

The novel binomials were selected to be absent from BabyLM's training
corpus, so they are genuinely novel only for the three BabyLM models;
indexed against Dolma \citep{dolma}, a trillion-token corpus, roughly
98.4\% occur at non-zero frequency. For the thirteen large-scale models,
then, the probe is trained on infrequent attested binomials, not unseen
ones. This is why Experiment 1 anchors the interpretation. Corpus
frequency is likewise an exact measure of what the model saw only for
BabyLM and Pythia; for GPT-2, OLMo, and Llama, Pile counts stand in as a
proxy for web-scale exposure.

The two sources in Equation~\ref{eq-relfreq} are also not measured on
equal footing, which is why we treat the ordering in Experiment 3 as
soft evidence. \(\hat{y}\) is a fitted readout of the model at that
checkpoint, so it tracks whatever the model currently encodes. Relative
frequency is a fixed corpus statistic measured with error, and
measurement error in a predictor pulls its coefficient toward zero. It
is also computed over the whole training corpus rather than over what
the model has seen by that checkpoint, so early on it describes text the
model has not encountered yet. Both asymmetries push storage later and
abstract preferences earlier, which is the direction our ordering runs.
The BabyLM checkpoints make the problem concrete: they sit at fixed
epoch fractions, and relative frequency becomes detectable at the first
checkpoint past one complete pass over the corpus in all three models.
That is what we would expect if storage requires repeated exposure, and
equally what we would expect if the predictor only starts describing the
model's experience after one pass. Our data cannot separate the two.

Additionally, the results are limited to English and to a single
construction.

Finally, the intervention separates additive from non-additive
information, not abstract from holistic information directly. The two
line up only if abstract constraints can be stated over each word on its
own. Most of the constraints in \citet{morgan2016} can be: relative word
length or frequency compares two quantities that each word carries by
itself, so an additive representation preserves them. A constraint over
the juncture between the two words is different. Avoiding a stress clash
across the boundary, for instance, is abstract and generalizable, but
the mask would likely remove it and we would count it as holistic
storage. The dissociation between the two effects suggests this isn't
what's driving our results, since losing information indiscriminately
would have weakened both effects rather than only one. Even so, the
design doesn't rule it out.

\section{Binomial Extraction Criteria}\label{sec-appendix-extraction}

Binomials (``W1 and W2'') were first selected using spaCy \citep{spacy2}
part-of-speech tags, retaining only cases where both \(W_1\) and \(W_2\)
are open-class words (nouns, verbs, adjectives, or adverbs). Each
candidate was then parsed with the Berkeley Neural Parser
\citep{kitaev-klein-2018-constituency, kitaev-etal-2019-multilingual} to
confirm that the two words form a minimal coordinate phrase. The
inclusion criterion was that the two words be co-constituents under the
same phrasal node (NP, VP, ADJP, ADVP, PP, or equivalent), with that
node spanning exactly the three tokens {[}\(W_1\), and, \(W_2\){]} and
nothing else. This ensures the extracted items are genuine coordinate
binomials rather than incidental sequences where ``and'' links clauses
or longer phrases. Novel binomials were extracted from Wikitext by the
identical procedure, with the added requirements that both words appear
in BabyLM's vocabulary and that the binomial is absent from the attested
set.

Because our extraction criteria requires W1 and W2 to form a
phrase-level constituent spanning exactly W1, ``and,'' and W2, binomials
that do not satisfy this constituency criterion are excluded. We do not
additionally require the two conjuncts to receive the same POS tag,
because spaCy's POS tagger can misclassify participial forms in
coordinate contexts, for example tagging ``mortified'' as VERB rather
than ADJ in ``disgusted and mortified.'' Requiring POS-tag agreement
would therefore risk excluding valid binomials because of POS-tagging
errors.

Two further restrictions apply to the items we probe. First, each
binomial must occur at least twice in Wikitext with at least ten
characters of preceding text, since we measure the representations in
one occurrence and the ordering preference in the other, and a binomial
at the start of a sentence has no left context to distinguish it from
any other such item. Second, reduplicatives (``again and again'') are
excluded throughout: their two orderings are the same string, so the
ordering preference is identically zero and carries no information. Of
the 340,042 novel binomials the extraction yields, 216,523 satisfy both
conditions, and it is these that the probes and the frequency regression
use. Retention is higher for frequent binomials than for rare ones, but
it is unrelated to which ordering a binomial prefers: among items
attested once in the training corpus, the mean relative frequency of
those retained is \(-0.004\) against \(-0.000\) for those dropped.

\section{Implementation Details}\label{sec-appendix-implementation}

\textbf{Probe training.} The probe is optimized with Adam (learning rate
\(10^{-3}\), weight decay \(10^{-4}\)) under mean-squared-error loss,
with early stopping (patience 5) against a 10\% held-out validation
split. Antisymmetric augmentation is applied with probability 0.5: for
each binomial the flipped version, with swapped ordering and negated
label, is added to the training batch. The input dimensionality follows
from the representation type.

\textbf{Attention-zeroing.} The zero weight is imposed before the
softmax, by adding a large negative constant to the corresponding
attention logits, so that the remaining positions renormalize to sum to
one.

\textbf{Recomputing the target.} Because \(y_{\text{true}}\) is the
model's own log-probability ratio, it is recomputed from whichever
forward pass produced the representations being probed. The two
full-context representation types therefore share a target, while the
attention-zeroed condition has its own: blocking cross-word attention
changes the model's output probabilities as well as its hidden states.

\section{Linear Probe Replication}\label{sec-appendix-linear}

The MLP probe has many free choices: hidden dimension, depth,
activation, learning rate, weight decay, patience, validation fraction,
augmentation probability, batch size, and initialization. A skeptical
reading is that some of them were tuned toward the reported result. We
therefore repeat the analysis with a probe that has one.

The linear probe is a ridge regression on the same features, fit on the
same cross-validation folds. Its only free variable is the ridge
penalty, selected per fold from \(\{10^0, \ldots, 10^5\}\) on an inner
split of that fold's training data. That inner split is the same size as
the MLP's early-stopping split, so both probes receive an identical
budget of held-out data for hyperparameter selection.

Across 16 model-checkpoint cells and 64 comparisons, the two probes
agree closely (\emph{r} = 0.985 between their R² values). On the
word-held-out split the linear probe recovers 103\% of the MLP's R² on
average (0.125 vs.~0.122).

\section{Cross-Validation Split Sizes}\label{sec-appendix-cv}

Cross-validation is carried out on the novel set only. The attested set
is never cross-validated: it is used solely as the transfer target
described in Section~\ref{sec-methods-analyses}. Both splits use ten
folds, and the same folds are reused across models, attention
conditions, and probe architectures so that all reported R² values are
directly comparable.

Under the binomial-held-out split, binomials are assigned to folds
directly, so each fold trains on nine tenths of the set and tests on the
remaining tenth. Under the word-held-out split it is the
\emph{vocabulary} that is partitioned into ten folds. A binomial enters
the test set for fold \(k\) only if both of its words are in fold \(k\),
and enters the training set only if neither word is. Binomials with one
word in fold \(k\) and one word outside it are used in neither, and are
discarded for that fold. Since a binomial needs both words drawn from
the same tenth of the vocabulary to be testable, the word-held-out test
set is roughly a hundredth of the set rather than a tenth.
Table~\ref{tbl-cv-sizes} gives the resulting sizes.

\section{Corpus Frequency Distributions}\label{sec-appendix-corpus-freq}

The figure below compares the frequency distributions of the 48,965
corpus binomials in the BabyLM training corpus (150M tokens, 20 epochs)
and the Pile, illustrating the difference in lexical coverage between
the two training regimes. BabyLM's distribution is dominated by
singleton binomials (71.2\% appear exactly once), while Pile frequencies
span several orders of magnitude.

\begin{figure}[H]

\centering{

\pandocbounded{\includegraphics[keepaspectratio]{writeup_files/figure-pdf/fig-corpus-freq-1.pdf}}

}

\caption{\label{fig-corpus-freq}Frequency distributions for the 48,965
corpus binomials in the BabyLM training corpus (150M tokens, 20 epochs)
and the Pile. Left: histogram of log\(_{10}\) raw counts. Right:
rank-frequency plot treating counts as a probability distribution
(probability = count / \(\sum\) counts); both axes log-scaled. BabyLM's
distribution is heavily concentrated at a single occurrence per pair
(71.2\% of pairs appear exactly once), whereas Pile frequencies span
several orders of magnitude with a median of 365 occurrences per pair.}

\end{figure}%

The BabyLM training corpus is a 150M-token collection of child-directed
and informal text (CHILDES, BNC Spoken, children's books, OpenSubtitles,
Wikipedia), trained for 20 epochs. The Pile is a
\textasciitilde300B-token web-scraped corpus used to train the Pythia
family. For each of the 48,965 corpus binomials, we extracted 3-gram
counts (i.e., the frequency of ``word\(_1\) and word\(_2\)'' and its
reverse) from each corpus via infini-gram \citep{liu2024infinigram}. As
Figure~\ref{fig-corpus-freq} shows, the BabyLM corpus produces a highly
skewed frequency distribution: 71.2\% of binomials appear exactly once,
87.7\% appear at most twice, and 98.2\% appear at most ten times, with a
maximum of 1,095 occurrences. The Pile distribution is far richer: the
median binomial appears 365 times, the 25th percentile is 62
occurrences, and the 99th percentile exceeds 77,000.

In absolute training exposure terms (accounting for BabyLM's 20 epochs),
the most frequent BabyLM binomial that enters our analyses, \emph{back
and forth}, is seen \textasciitilde21,600 times total across all
training steps, while the same binomial is seen over 1.5 million times
in a single pass of the Pile. Even the median Pile binomial (365
occurrences per pass) substantially exceeds BabyLM's total exposure for
a typical singleton binomial (1 × 20 = 20 exposures).

\section{Frequency-Dependent Preference
Regression}\label{sec-appendix-relfreq}

Full results for Equation~\ref{eq-relfreq}: abstract preferences
\(\hat{y}\) (\texttt{y\_pred}), the binomial's relative frequency in the
corpus (\texttt{rel\_freq}), overall binomial frequency
(\texttt{log\_freq}), the two moderations, and the residual-scale term.

We first justify the z-scoring of \texttt{y\_true} and \texttt{y\_pred}
and \emph{not} z-scoring \texttt{rel\_freq} and \texttt{log\_freq}.
\texttt{y\_true} and \texttt{y\_pred} are properties of the model: both
are log-probability ratios, so a unit is nominally the same everywhere,
but the spread of that quantity reflects how sharp a given model's
output distribution is rather than how much it knows about binomials.
Across the models here the standard deviation of \texttt{y\_true} ranges
from 2.79 to 4.48, and it bears almost no relation to probe R²
(\(r = 0.15\) across the sixteen models, and it runs in the opposite
direction within BabyLM), so a raw coefficient would partly measure
output calibration. We therefore z-score both within each fit, which
puts every coefficient in units of that model's own preference spread.

\texttt{rel\_freq} and \texttt{log\_freq}, by contrast, are properties
of the training corpus: a proportion of occurrences and a count of them
mean the same thing however they are measured, and dividing either by a
corpus-specific standard deviation would replace a shared unit with a
local one. They are therefore left on their own scales:
\texttt{rel\_freq} is a proportion running from \(-0.5\) to \(+0.5\) and
\texttt{log\_freq} is centered but not scaled, so both mean the same
thing in every corpus.

\subsection{Cross-Model Agreement on Novel
Binomials}\label{sec-appendix-agreement}

A binomial absent from the training corpus cannot have been memorized
(definitionally), but the model still assigns a preference, and that
value is some deterministic function of the two words' representations.
Such a function could reflect systematic knowledge, or it could be an
arbitrary consequence of a particular set of weights. Probe
generalization cannot distinguish these, because both are systematic
functions of the input and both transfer to held-out words. Agreement
between independently trained models can: an arbitrary function of one
model's weights has no reason to match another model's preferences.

We therefore correlate \(y_{\text{true}}\) across models on identical
binomials, separately for the attested and novel sets and for both
attention conditions. As a baseline we shuffle one model's values, which
removes any pairing between models while preserving both distributions.

The three BabyLM models are the decisive comparison. They share a
training corpus and its ordering but differ in capacity, and they are
the only models for which the novel binomials are genuinely absent from
training (Section~\ref{sec-exp2}, Methods). On novel binomials they
agree at \(r = 0.50\) under full context and \(r = 0.49\) under
attention-zeroing, against a shuffled baseline of \(r \approx 0.00\). In
other words, preferences for binomials the models never encountered are
reproducible across models of different sizes trained on the same data.
They are therefore not arbitrary.

Because a Pearson correlation can in principle be carried by a minority
of high-magnitude items, we checked whether the agreement is
concentrated in a handful of binomials with strong preferences. This is
not the case. Preferences on novel binomials are not weak to begin with:
median \(|y_{\text{true}}|\) ranges from 1.65 to 3.21 across the three
models and both attention conditions.

Rank correlations track the Pearson values closely (0.36--0.55 for
BabyLM on novel binomials), and the models order the same binomial the
same way on 60\% to 70\% of items against a chance rate of 50\%, a
statistic that ignores magnitude and weights every binomial equally.

Two further patterns appear in Table~\ref{tbl-agreement}. Agreement
within a family is roughly constant across all four cells, between
\(r = 0.77\) and \(0.81\), and agreement between families is nearly as
stable, between \(0.62\) and \(0.67\). Both hold up on novel binomials
and under attention-zeroing: what the models share is confined neither
to binomials they were trained on nor to models that share a training
corpus. The within-family advantage nonetheless appears in every cell,
which rules out the shared sentence frame as the source of the
agreement: the frame is identical for all sixteen models, so it cannot
produce a gap between within-family and between-family agreement.

\subsection{Word-Level Replication}\label{sec-appendix-words-only-exp1}

We trained the MLP probe using word-level representations for the three
BabyLM models to verify that the preference signal is not an artifact of
mean pooling across the full binomial phrase. Under this condition the
probe receives a concatenation of two word vectors extracted from
isolated sentence contexts (``\emph{the bread}'', ``\emph{the
butter}''), so it cannot rely on any co-occurrence signal from the two
words' shared sentence context.

Binomial-held-out R² ranges from 0.07 to 0.13, matching the
corresponding binomial-level values (0.07--0.13). The generalization
pattern is preserved: word-held-out R² is consistently lower than
binomial-held-out R² for all three models (word-held-out 0.06--0.09;
binomial-minus-word gap 0.018--0.041), confirming that the lexical
generalization cost seen at the binomial level is a property of the
lexical representation itself rather than an artifact of the mean-pooled
representation. The frequency-dependent preference regression was fit at
the binomial level only, so it has no word-level counterpart. Full
results are in Table~\ref{tbl-r2-babylm-words-only}.

\section{Tokenization Artifact at
Initialization}\label{sec-appendix-tokenization}

At step 0 the untrained Pythia models show a small but credible effect
of relative frequency (Table~\ref{tbl-tokenization}). This appendix
shows that the effect is an artifact of subword tokenization rather than
any ordering preference in the untrained networks, and that removing it
leaves the trained models unchanged.

Where a binomial's left context ends in a quotation mark, parenthesis,
or hyphen rather than whitespace, whichever word comes first loses its
leading space, and byte-pair encoding segments many words differently
without one. The two orderings then sum different numbers of span
tokens. At initialization every token costs roughly the log of the
vocabulary size, so a one-token difference is worth about 11 nats for
Pythia, swamping a typical ordering preference of well under one. This
affects 571 of the 27,835 corpus binomials (2.1\%): 53\% of those whose
context ends in a quotation mark against 0.1\% of those ending in
whitespace.

We refit Equation~\ref{eq-relfreq} for the three Pythia models at step 0
and at their final checkpoint, excluding those 571 items. Everything
else is held fixed, and the scaling is recomputed on the retained rows.
Table~\ref{tbl-tokenization} gives the relative-frequency coefficient
before and after. At step 0 the effect is credible in all six cells as
reported and in none of them once the items are dropped. At the final
checkpoint the coefficient moves by at most 0.007, which is well inside
its credible interval. The artifact dominates at step 0 only because
there is no real signal competing with it: those 571 items carry roughly
80\% of the variance in an untrained model's preferences and about 3\%
in a trained one.
