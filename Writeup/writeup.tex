\section{Introduction}\label{introduction}

\section{Experiment 1: BabyLM Representations}\label{sec-exp1}

In this first experiment, we probe three BabyLM models (125M, 350M, and
1.3B parameters)\footnote{The BabyLM models used here are OPT-based
  models trained for 20 epochs on a 150M-token subset of the BabyLM
  benchmark corpus; \citet{houghton2026holisticstorageverbupphrases};
  \citet{warstadt-etal-2023-findings}{]}.} at their final training
checkpoint. These three models were trained on an amount of data
comparable to humans and thus are more directly comparable to the human
behavioral literature. Because the binomials used in this study were
selected to be absent from BabyLM's training corpus, this experiment
provides the cleanest test of whether a language model encodes abstract
binomial ordering preferences: any preference signal the probe recovers
must have been generalized from the training distribution rather than
read off from direct exposure to the test pairs.

\subsection{Methods}\label{methods}

Below we describe the methods used in Experiment 1.

\subsubsection{Stimuli}\label{stimuli}

We use two complementary datasets of binomial expressions (\emph{X and
Y}). Binomials are a strong test case because they are well-studied in
the linguistics literature \citep[e.g.,][]{morgan2016, benor2006} and
human ordering preferences for them are driven by abstract preferences
\citep{morgan2015, morgan2016}. The \textbf{novel set} comprises
approximately 340,000 binomial pairs from the Wikitext corpus
\citep{merity2016pointer} selected to be absent from BabyLM's training
corpus. The novel pairs are therefore genuinely novel for the BabyLM
models used in Experiment 1.

The \textbf{attested corpus set} comprises approximately 49,000 binomial
pairs from the BabyLM corpus \citep{warstadt-etal-2023-findings}. For
each corpus pair and each model, the ordering preference is
operationalized as the model's own log-probability ratio:
\(y_{\text{true}} = \log P(W_1\text{ and }W_2) - \log P(W_2\text{ and }W_1)\).

\subsubsection{Representation
Extraction}\label{representation-extraction}

For each model, we extract hidden-state representations from the final
hidden-layer. Each binomial pair is presented to the model in both
orderings (\emph{W}\textsubscript{1} and \emph{W}\textsubscript{2};
\emph{W}\textsubscript{2} and \emph{W}\textsubscript{1}) within a
natural sentence frame. We extract representations under two input
modes:

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{Binomial-level} (\texttt{mean\_pooled}): the input to the
  probe is
  \([\bar{h}(W_1\text{ and }W_2); \bar{h}(W_2\text{ and }W_1)]\), where
  \(\bar{h}\) denotes the mean of token representations across all
  tokens in the binomial in each ordering.
\item
  \textbf{Word-level} (\texttt{words\_only}): the input is
  \([h(W_1); h(W_2)]\), where \(h(W_k)\) is the hidden state of word
  \(W_k\) extracted from the sentence in which it appears in first
  position, excluding the conjunction token.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Attention Conditions}\label{attention-conditions}

We probe representations under two attention conditions:

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{Full context} (\texttt{default}): standard forward pass with
  full cross-token attention. Each word's representation reflects
  contextual influences from all other tokens in the sentence.
\item
  \textbf{Attention-zeroed} (\texttt{attn\_zeroed}): attention from the
  second word and the conjunction to the first word is blocked by
  setting the corresponding attention logits to \(-\infty\) before the
  softmax. This condition tests whether the second word's representation
  is specifically shaped by direct cross-word attention to the first
  word.
\end{itemize}

Because the word-level mode extracts each word from the sentence in
which it occupies first position, its representation depends only on the
preceding sentence context and is not influenced by any later token's
attention. We therefore report three representation types per model:
full-context binomial-level, attention-zeroed binomial-level, and
full-context word-level.

\subsubsection{MLP Probe}\label{mlp-probe}

For each representation type, we train a two-layer MLP classifier
(hidden dimension 128, ReLU activation) to predict binomial preference
from the extracted representations. The probe takes as its input the
concatenated hidden-layer representations for a given binomial
(dimensionality and construction determined by the representation type,
as operationalized above) and outputs a single continuous value
\(\hat{y}\), trained via mean-squared-error loss to approximate the true
preference score, \(y_{true}\), defined in the Stimuli section. The
model is trained with Adam (learning rate \(10^{-3}\), weight decay
\(10^{-4}\)) with early stopping (patience = 20) and a 10\% held-out
validation set. Training uses antisymmetric augmentation: for each pair,
we add a flipped version (swapped ordering, negated label) with
probability 0.5. We report cross-validated R² (the squared Pearson
correlation between \(\hat{y}\) and \(y_{true}\) on held-out folds) as
our measure of probe performance, averaged across 10 folds.

We evaluate generalization under two cross-validation splits:

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{Pair-held-out} (\texttt{pair\_novel}): pairs are held out as
  units; both orderings of a pair appear only in training or only in
  test.
\item
  \textbf{Word-held-out} (\texttt{word\_novel}): all pairs containing a
  test word are excluded from training, testing generalization to
  completely novel lexical items.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Control Probes}\label{control-probes}

Following \citet{hewitt-liang-2019-designing}, we train a matched
control probe on randomly permuted preference labels under the same
architecture and hyperparameters. Near-zero control R² provides evidence
that the probe is extracting genuine linguistic structure rather than
exploiting label artifacts or probe capacity. Full control R² results
are reported in appendix sections alongside each experiment.

\subsubsection{Frequency-Residual
Regression}\label{sec-methods-freq-regression}

To characterize how corpus frequency relates to probe accuracy, we
regress the absolute residual error of the MLP probe on log corpus
frequency using a Bayesian quadratic regression model implemented in
\emph{brms} \citep{burkner2017brms} with default priors. Frequency was
estimated using exact counts of the binomial in the BabyLM training
corpus \citep{warstadt-etal-2023-findings}. Our statistical model
equation is presented below:

\begin{equation}\phantomsection\label{eq-freq-regression}{|y_{\text{true}} - \hat{y}| \sim \beta_0 + \beta_1 \log(\text{freq}) + \beta_2 \log(\text{freq})^2}\end{equation}

\subsection{Results}\label{results}

\subsubsection{Probe Performance}\label{probe-performance}

Results are visualized in Figure~\ref{fig-r2-babylm}; full numerical
results are in Appendix \ref{sec-appendix-exp1}. The control probe R²
values were consistently near zero across all conditions and all three
models (mean ± SD = 0.0001 ± 0.0001; full control results are in
Appendix \ref{sec-appendix-exp1}), confirming that the probe doesn't
recover spurious signal from label noise.

For the binomial-level conditions, pair-held-out R² ranges from 0.24 to
0.34; word-held-out R² is consistently lower, ranging from 0.12 to 0.23.
The drop from pair-held-out to word-held-out reflects a lexical
generalization cost: the probe is more accurate when the words in a test
pair appeared in other pairs during probe training than when both words
are entirely novel, suggesting that some preference signal is tied to
word identity rather than being fully abstract. Probe accuracy is
non-monotonic with model size: BabyLM-350M achieves the highest
pair-held-out R² across most conditions, with BabyLM-125M and
BabyLM-1.3B lower by similar margins. The full-context condition yields
higher pair-held-out R² than attention-zeroed, indicating that
cross-word attention contributes positively to preference encoding for
these novel binomials.

\subsubsection{Memorization
vs.~Abstraction}\label{memorization-vs.-abstraction}

Full numerical results and fitted curves are in Appendix
\ref{sec-appendix-exp1}. Under the full-context condition, the quadratic
coefficient \(\hat\beta_2\) is positive for all three models, with 95\%
credible intervals excluding zero. This indicates a U-shaped
relationship between corpus frequency and probe error: the probe makes
the smallest errors for pairs at intermediate log-frequency values, with
errors increasing toward both the low- and high-frequency tails. Under
the attention-zeroed condition, the curvature is attenuated relative to
full context and remains reliably positive for BabyLM-125M and
BabyLM-350M; BabyLM-1.3B is a marginal exception
(\(\hat\beta_2 = 0.008\); 75\% of posterior above zero). The vertex of
the quadratic --- the log-frequency value at which probe error is
minimized --- falls within the intermediate range of the observed
frequency distribution across all models and conditions. The attenuation
under the attention-zeroed condition indicates that the high-frequency
penalty on probe accuracy is driven in part by cross-word attention:
blocking the second word's attention to the first flattens the
frequency-residual relationship, suggesting that this cross-word
interaction contributes specifically to the pair-specific encoding that
degrades probe accuracy at high frequencies.

\section{Experiment 2: Cross-Family Final-Layer
Representations}\label{sec-exp2}

\subsection{Methods}\label{methods-1}

We probe all 16 models spanning BabyLM, Pythia, GPT-2, OLMo, and Llama
families (117M to 8B parameters, trained on 150M to approximately 15T
tokens) at their final training checkpoints. One design consideration
differs from Experiment 1: the novel binomials were selected to be
absent from BabyLM's training corpus, but they are not truly novel for
the Pile-trained models --- approximately 98\% appear in the Pile at
non-zero frequency, most at low-to-medium exposure (Pile-equivalent
counts spanning 14 to 443 occurrences for the middle 50\% of items). The
probe R² results for these models therefore reflect how representations
of low-frequency attested pairs compare to higher-frequency ones within
each family, rather than generalization from attested to genuinely
unseen items; validity checks are discussed in
Section~\ref{sec-exp2-validity}. Probe architecture, attention
conditions, and frequency regression are identical to Experiment 1.

\subsection{Results}\label{results-1}

\subsubsection{Probe Performance}\label{probe-performance-1}

Full numerical results are in Appendix Table~\ref{tbl-r2};
Figure~\ref{fig-r2} visualizes the pair-held-out results. Control probe
R² was consistently near zero across all models and conditions (mean ±
SD = 0.0002 ± 0.0002; full control results in Appendix
\ref{sec-appendix-exp2}). As in Experiment 1, the binomial-level
conditions (full-context and attention-zeroed) are directly comparable;
word-level results are provided in Appendix \ref{sec-appendix-exp2}. All
Pile-trained models substantially outperform BabyLM: pair-held-out R²
under the full-context, binomial-level condition ranges from 0.43 to
0.65 across the four Pile-trained families, compared to 0.29--0.34 for
BabyLM. Word-held-out R² is consistently lower than pair-held-out R² for
every model and condition, matching the pattern in Experiment 1. GPT-2
shows the most striking result: under the attention-zeroed condition,
pair-held-out R² reaches 0.79 for GPT-2 (117M) --- substantially higher
than its full-context value of 0.65 --- with attention-condition
advantages across all four GPT-2 models: GPT-2-M (0.76 vs.~0.53),
GPT-2-L (0.67 vs.~0.52), and GPT-2-XL (0.73 vs.~0.49). Pythia, OLMo, and
Llama-1.3B show the same direction: attention-zeroed outperforms full
context at the binomial level. Llama-3-8B is an exception, matching the
BabyLM pattern with full context slightly outperforming attention-zeroed
(0.54 vs.~0.52). Scale effects within families are non-monotonic: in
GPT-2, R² decreases with model size under full context (117M highest at
0.65, XL lowest at 0.49); in Pythia, the pattern is non-monotonic (160M
highest at 0.59, 410M lowest at 0.53, 1B at 0.55); OLMo's 7B models
slightly outperform 1B counterparts; Llama's 8B model outperforms 1.3B.

\subsubsection{Memorization
vs.~Abstraction}\label{memorization-vs.-abstraction-1}

Full numerical results are in Appendix Table~\ref{tbl-beta} and
visualized in Figure~\ref{fig-beta}. \(\hat{\beta}_2\) is positive for
all 16 models under both attention conditions, with 95\% credible
intervals excluding zero in all cases except BabyLM-1.3B under the
attention-zeroed condition (\(\hat{\beta}_2 = 0.008\); 75\% of posterior
above zero). The U-shaped frequency-residual relationship is therefore a
robust finding across model families, training corpora, and parameter
scales. Two patterns stand out. First, BabyLM models show larger
\(\hat{\beta}_2\) magnitudes under full context (0.064--0.073) than any
Pile-trained family (0.007--0.018), despite far less training --- an
effect likely driven by BabyLM's extreme frequency distribution (70\%
singleton pairs), which creates a sharper contrast between low- and
high-frequency items in the regression. Second, under the
attention-zeroed condition, \(\hat{\beta}_2\) is consistently attenuated
relative to full context across all families, confirming that cross-word
attention contributes to the high-frequency memorization effect
regardless of model family or scale.

\subsection{Validity}\label{sec-exp2-validity}

Two validity concerns arise for the Experiment 2 frequency-residual
results. First, the model's own ordering preference
(\(y_{\text{true}}\)) is uncorrelated with corpus frequency for all 16
models (all \(|r| < 0.03\)), ruling out range restriction as an
explanation for the U-shape: the signal the probe must predict is
equally distributed across frequency levels. Second, word-held-out R²
closely matches pair-held-out R² for all 14 Pile-trained models
(Appendix \ref{sec-appendix-exp2}), demonstrating that the high R²
values reflect a generalizable mapping from representation to preference
rather than a lookup over memorized pairs. Figure~\ref{fig-scatter}
(Appendix) corroborates this visually: mid-frequency pairs cluster
tightly around the prediction diagonal, while low- and high-frequency
pairs scatter in opposite directions --- precisely the pattern implied
by positive \(\hat{\beta}_2\).

Experiments 1 and 2 establish what models encode at the end of training.
Experiment 3 turns to training dynamics: we ask when during training
these representations emerge, and how the frequency-residual
relationship evolves with additional exposure.

\section{Experiment 3: Developmental Trajectories}\label{sec-exp3}

Experiments 1 and 2 establish that language models encode binomial
preferences at the end of training, but they leave open when during
training these representations emerge, and whether the
memorization-abstraction tradeoff is present from early on or
crystallizes with additional exposure. Experiment 3 addresses this by
examining the same probe at multiple training checkpoints. We ask two
questions: (1) Does probe accuracy increase monotonically with training,
or does it plateau early, suggesting that preference representations are
rapidly acquired? (2) Does the U-shaped frequency-residual relationship
hold from early checkpoints, or does it only emerge as training
progresses and memorization of high-frequency items accumulates?

\subsection{Methods}\label{methods-2}

We probe six BabyLM checkpoints per model size (125M, 350M, 1.3B),
sampled at approximately even intervals across training. For Pythia, we
use the publicly released intermediate checkpoints at steps 16, 32, 64,
256, 512, and 1000 for the 160M, 410M, and 1B models. Pythia steps are
indexed to gradient updates; each step corresponds to approximately 2.1M
tokens, so these six checkpoints span roughly 34M to 2.1B tokens of
exposure --- approximately the first 0.7\% of the full 300B-token
training run. Because no later intermediate checkpoints are publicly
released for Pythia, the Pythia trajectory analysis is limited to this
early-training window; the fully trained models analyzed in Experiments
1 and 2 represent the end of 300B tokens of training. BabyLM checkpoints
span steps 24--2280 (125M), 48--4560 (350M), and 97--9021 (1.3B),
corresponding to approximately 20M to 1.9B effective training tokens for
each model size (each step processes a fixed number of tokens: 819K for
125M, 410K for 350M, 205K for 1.3B).

The probe, input modes, attention conditions, and cross-validation
splits are identical to Experiment 1. For the frequency-residual
analysis, we fit the quadratic Bayesian regression described in
Experiment 1 at each checkpoint and report \(\hat\beta_2\) (the
curvature of the U-shape) and the vertex log-frequency (the point at
which probe error is minimized) as a function of training step.

\subsection{Results}\label{results-2}

BabyLM pair-held-out R² is already high at the earliest checkpoint
(\textasciitilde20M tokens: 0.67--0.72) and broadly declines to
0.32--0.38 by the end of training. At the earliest checkpoint, the
attention-zeroed condition outperforms full context (0.78--0.80
vs.~0.67--0.72); this ordering reverses between \textasciitilde120M and
\textasciitilde750M tokens, so that full context exceeds
attention-zeroed at the final checkpoint for all three model sizes.
Pythia shows an inverted-U trajectory instead: R² rises from 0.68--0.74
at 33.6M tokens to a peak of 0.80--0.86 at 537M tokens, then declines to
0.71--0.76 at 1074M--2097M tokens --- consistent with Pile-trained
models still consolidating preference representations during this early
window (the first 0.7\% of 300B tokens). The attention condition flip
for Pythia follows the same qualitative pattern as BabyLM. Full
numerical results are in Appendix \ref{sec-appendix-exp3}; word-level
results are in Appendix \ref{sec-appendix-exp3}.

For \(\hat{\beta}_2\), BabyLM starts negative at all sizes (-0.013 to
-0.029), remains negative until approximately 750M tokens, and reaches
0.045--0.093 at the final checkpoint, matching the positive curvature
from Experiment 1. Pythia's \(\hat{\beta}_2\) is positive from its
earliest checkpoint (33.6M tokens), with credible intervals confidently
excluding zero by 537M tokens. As discussed in Appendix
\ref{sec-appendix-corpus-freq}, this contrast reflects the far greater
absolute token exposure per pair in the Pile relative to BabyLM. Full
numerical results are in Appendix \ref{sec-appendix-exp3}.

\section{Discussion}\label{sec-discussion}

All 16 models encode decodable binomial ordering preferences in their
final-layer representations, with probe R² well above near-zero control
values in every condition. BabyLM models produce lower R² (0.24--0.34)
than their Pile-trained counterparts (0.43--0.79), consistent with their
smaller training corpora, and scale effects within families are
non-monotonic. Across Pile-trained families, attention-zeroed
outperforms full-context at the binomial level --- indicating that
cross-token attention in data-rich models encodes pair-specific,
idiosyncratic representations that reduce probe generalizability ---
while BabyLM shows the reverse, consistent with attention computing
abstract compositional features rather than retrieving memorized pair
orderings. Positive quadratic curvature of probe error over
log-frequency (\(\hat{\beta}_2 > 0\)) holds across all models and
conditions, revealing a mid-frequency advantage in which pairs at
intermediate frequencies are easiest to decode, with both rare pairs
(insufficient signal) and highly frequent pairs (idiosyncratic
memorization) producing elevated prediction error.

\subsection{Word-Level Replication}\label{sec-appendix-words-only-exp1}

We trained the MLP probe using word-level representations
(\texttt{words\_only}) for the three BabyLM models to verify that the
preference signal is not an artifact of mean pooling across the full
binomial phrase. Under this condition the probe receives a concatenation
of two word vectors extracted from isolated sentence contexts
(``\emph{the bread}'', ``\emph{the butter}''), so it cannot rely on any
co-occurrence signal from the two words' shared sentence context.

Pair-held-out R² ranges from 0.31 to 0.33 --- within a few percentage
points of the corresponding binomial-level values (0.29--0.34). The
generalization pattern is preserved: word-held-out R² is consistently
lower than pair-held-out R² for all three models (word-held-out
0.18--0.20; pair-minus-word gap 0.127--0.129), confirming that the
lexical generalization cost seen at the binomial level is a property of
the preference encoding itself rather than an artifact of the
mean-pooled representation. \(\hat{\beta}_2\) is positive and credible
intervals exclude zero for all three models, matching the curvature
found at the binomial level (words-only \(\hat{\beta}_2\): 0.039--0.079;
full-context binomial-level: 0.064--0.073). Full results are in

\subsection{Word-Level Replication}\label{sec-appendix-words-only-exp3}

Word-level probe results across BabyLM and Pythia checkpoints replicate
the key patterns from the binomial-level analysis. The generalization
gap (pair-held-out minus word-held-out) is largest at the earliest

\section{Corpus Frequency Distributions}\label{sec-appendix-corpus-freq}

The figure below compares the frequency distributions of the
approximately 47,000 corpus binomials in the BabyLM training corpus
(150M tokens, 20 epochs) and the Pile, illustrating the stark difference
in lexical coverage between the two training regimes. BabyLM's
distribution is dominated by singleton pairs (70.7\% appear exactly
once), while Pile frequencies span several orders of magnitude.

\begin{figure}

\centering{

\pandocbounded{\includegraphics[keepaspectratio]{writeup_files/figure-pdf/fig-corpus-freq-1.pdf}}

}

\caption{\label{fig-corpus-freq}Frequency distributions for the
\textasciitilde47K corpus binomials in the BabyLM training corpus (150M
tokens, 20 epochs) and the Pile. Left: histogram of log\(_{10}\) raw
counts. Right: rank-frequency plot treating counts as a probability
distribution (probability = count / \(\sum\) counts); both axes
log-scaled. BabyLM's distribution is heavily concentrated at a single
occurrence per pair (70.7\% of pairs appear exactly once), whereas Pile
frequencies span several orders of magnitude with a median of 365
occurrences per pair.}

\end{figure}%

The BabyLM training corpus is a 150M-token collection of child-directed
and informal text (CHILDES, BNC Spoken, children's books, OpenSubtitles,
Wikipedia), trained for 20 epochs. The Pile is a
\textasciitilde300B-token web-scraped corpus used to train the Pythia
family. For each of the \textasciitilde47K corpus binomials, we
extracted 3-gram counts (i.e., the frequency of ``word\(_1\) and
word\(_2\)'' and its reverse) from each corpus via infinigram. As
Figure~\ref{fig-corpus-freq} shows, the BabyLM corpus produces a highly
degenerate frequency distribution: 70.7\% of pairs appear exactly once,
87.4\% appear at most twice, and 98.2\% appear at most ten times, with a
maximum of 1,095 occurrences. The Pile distribution is far richer: the
median pair appears 365 times, the 25th percentile is 62 occurrences,
and the 99th percentile exceeds 77,000.

In absolute training exposure terms (accounting for BabyLM's 20 epochs),
the most frequent BabyLM binomials are seen at most
\textasciitilde21,900 times total across all training steps, while the
equivalent Pile binomials are seen over 1.5 million times in a single
pass. Even the median Pile pair (365 occurrences per pass) substantially
exceeds BabyLM's total exposure for a typical singleton pair (1 × 20 =
20 exposures). This gap in absolute exposure is the proximate
explanation for why idiosyncratic memorization --- reflected in positive
\(\hat{\beta}_2\) --- emerges immediately in Pythia but requires
hundreds of millions of tokens to appear in BabyLM.
