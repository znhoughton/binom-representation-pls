# Sketch: Do Networks Represent Properties or Store Answers?

**Status:** parked. Revisit after the binomials paper is settled.
**Relation to the main paper:** independent. Uses none of the binomials pipeline, only the
conceptual framing.
**Last revised:** 2026-08-15

---

## The question, stated precisely

A standard objection to probing: recovering some quantity from a model's representations may
only show that the model has stored the *answer* on some direction, not that it represents the
structure producing the answer. From a probe alone the two are indistinguishable.

The distinction that matters is **property-derived versus answer-carrying**, and it is not the
same as "one direction versus many":

- **Acceptable.** A single shared direction whose value is computed from word properties, for
  example shorter-first or more-frequent-first. Linguistically meaningful even though it is one axis.
- **The concern.** A direction that simply carries the outcome, with no property basis. Arbitrary
  storage in the shape of a representation.

"Possible" and "what actually happens" are different claims. Given a task whose answer genuinely
is determined by abstract properties, does a trained network end up representing those properties
separably, or does it collapse them into a scalar answer axis? That is an empirical question about
inductive bias.

## Why it cannot be answered by simulating representations

This was tried (`Scripts/simulation_validate_measures.py`) and the design is circular for this
question. Synthesising representations requires hand-specifying the encoding, which decides the
answer in advance. Encoding a lexical bias on a shared axis makes it readable for held-out words;
encoding it word-specifically does not. Both were choices, not findings.

**The networks have to be trained.**

## Why it is worth a paper

It converts an unfalsifiable-in-principle objection into a measurement:

> We trained N models on data where the answer is known to be property-derived. In M of them the
> properties are separably encoded; in K the representation collapses to a scalar answer axis.

High M/N makes the readout account a theoretical edge case rather than a live concern for
interpreting probe results. Low M/N is a genuine warning to the probing literature. Either is
publishable.

## Design

**Synthetic language.** Vocabulary where each word carries known properties (length, frequency,
a phonology-like feature). Ordering in some target construction, binomials being the natural
choice, is generated by a known rule over those properties, plus a controlled set of memorised
exceptions that violate it.

**Training.** Small transformers on next-token prediction, 2–4 layers, small vocab. Cheap enough
to train dozens per condition on one GPU.

**Discovery, with ground truth in hand.**
- Are the individual properties independently decodable from the representations?
- Does the answer direction lie inside the span of the property directions (property-derived) or
  largely orthogonal to it (arbitrary storage)? The span test is the geometric version of the
  question and does not require the property inventory to be complete, only that the properties
  you do have are decodable.
- Does ablating a property direction shift predictions the way the rule says it should?

**Sweep.** Seeds, model size, corpus size, and most importantly the **proportion of memorised
exceptions** and **per-item frequency**.

## The finding to aim for

Not a single number but the **boundary**: the data conditions under which networks stop encoding
properties and start storing answers. Then ask where realistic LM training sits relative to it.

The prediction worth testing is that answer-storage grows with the exception rate and with
per-item frequency, which would connect directly to the main paper's result that probe error rises
at the high-frequency tail and that blocking cross-word attention reverses the frequency slope.

## What the measure-validation run already established

From `Scripts/simulation_validate_measures.py`, generating three components (feature-based
abstraction α, non-feature lexical bias λ, pair-specific memory μ) and checking what each measure
reports:

- **β₂ is highly selective for pair-specific memory.** r = +0.95 with μ, +0.05 with α, −0.14 with λ,
  and monotonic in μ. The frequency-residual measure does what the main paper claims it does.
- **Word-held-out R² is not selective.** It responds to feature-based abstraction (+0.67) *and* to
  arbitrary per-word storage (+0.52) when the latter sits on a shared readable axis. This is
  precisely the ambiguity the proposed paper would resolve.
- **The pair-versus-word gap was flat** regardless of λ, because a shared-axis encoding is equally
  readable under both CV splits. That the real data *does* show a gap implies the real encoding is
  not purely shared, which is itself an argument worth developing.

## Methodological findings worth reporting alongside

Accumulated while building the main paper's analyses; all negative results, all reusable:

- **PLS component sweeps do not measure intrinsic dimensionality.** On a signal one-dimensional by
  construction, R² still climbs from 0.83 at K=1 to 0.96 at K=6, because PLS components maximise
  covariance in the standardised space and finite-sample noise dilutes the first. The bias is
  constant, so cross-condition comparison remains valid; absolute claims do not.
- **Prefix priming.** Using naturally occurring sentences as frames means the prefix predicts the
  first word, inflating absolute accuracy. Within-item contrasts are immune; level estimates are not.
- **Residual bundling.** The residual of a per-word model contains compositional abstraction and
  context effects as well as memorisation, so its level is uninterpretable. Only its gradient
  against exposure is.
- **Stratum incomparability.** Frequency strata differ in composition, not just exposure, so
  cross-stratum comparisons need either matching or a within-item contrast.

## Caveats to state up front

- Toy-model inductive bias does not transfer automatically to billion-parameter models on natural
  corpora. The claim is "here is what networks do under conditions we can control."
- The property set must be genuinely learnable but not trivially so, or the result is rigged by
  the design rather than discovered.
